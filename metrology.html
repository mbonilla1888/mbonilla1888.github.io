Metrology, Materials Characterization & Measurement Systems

My metrology experience spans electrical measurements, electron microscopy, photoelectron spectroscopy, atomic-scale microscopy, magnetic characterization, optical metrology, environmental testing, and coating durability.

That experience progressed from using characterization equipment in early research to becoming the person responsible for operating, maintaining, troubleshooting, calibrating, and supporting complex measurement systems in graduate research and manufacturing.

Manufacturing Metrology

From Measurement User to Metrology SME

Measurement and characterization have been part of my technical work from undergraduate research through graduate surface-science research and industrial manufacturing.

Early work focused on using established measurement systems to understand electronic devices and materials. During graduate research, that expanded into direct ownership of sophisticated surface-analysis and microscopy equipment. In industry, I applied the same measurement discipline to optical manufacturing, process control, equipment troubleshooting, qualification, and durability testing.

  • Scanning Tunneling Microscopy (STM) and spectroscopy

  • Measurement-equipment troubleshooting and calibration

Early Electrical & Materials Characterization

My first metrology experience came through undergraduate research involving semiconductor devices, nanomaterials, and electronic materials. At this stage I primarily worked as a measurement-system user, learning how experimental data connects device behavior with material properties.

  • Current-voltage (I-V) characterization of transistors and diodes
  • Electrical continuity, open/short, and device-response measurements
  • Electrical characterization of transistor-based sensor structures
  • Scanning Electron Microscopy of electrospun nanofibers
  • Characterization of nanomaterials used in electronic and sensing devices

This early experience established a measurement-first approach that continued into later process-engineering and manufacturing work: understand what the process produced through data rather than relying only on equipment settings or visual inspection.

Photoelectron Spectroscopy & Electronic Structure

Early research also introduced me to surface-sensitive spectroscopy for understanding the occupied and unoccupied electronic structure of materials.

XPS

X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) for elemental composition, chemical-state information, core-level behavior, oxidation, chemical shifts, and surface chemistry.

UPS

Ultraviolet Photoelectron Spectroscopy (UPS) for valence electronic structure, occupied states, work-function-related measurements, and energy-level alignment.

IPES

Inverse Photoemission Spectroscopy (IPES) for studying unoccupied electronic states and combining occupied and unoccupied spectra to understand electronic band structure and band gaps.

UHV Surface Analysis

Measurements performed in Ultra-High Vacuum (UHV), developing early experience with surface cleanliness, vacuum-system behavior, sample preparation, and surface-sensitive measurements.

Undergraduate research included photoelectron and inverse-photoemission studies of inorganic and organic materials, including electronic band structure and periodic-poling effects.

Graduate Surface Science — XPS & UPS

During graduate research, XPS and UPS became core characterization techniques for the thin films I was growing and studying. My responsibility extended well beyond submitting samples for measurement.

I operated and maintained the spectroscopy system, collected the measurements, supported installation and troubleshooting, and developed deep expertise interpreting the resulting spectra.

  • Elemental and surface composition
  • Core-level spectroscopy
  • Chemical shifts and chemical-state analysis
  • Surface oxidation and contamination
  • Valence-band structure
  • Electronic states and energy levels
  • Band alignment and surface electronic behavior
  • Thin-film and interface characterization
  • Ultra-high-vacuum sample handling and measurement
  • Equipment troubleshooting, maintenance, and operational recovery

These measurements were directly connected to my deposition research, allowing film-growth conditions to be evaluated through the resulting chemistry and electronic structure.

Scanning Tunneling Microscopy & Atomic-Scale Characterization

Scanning Tunneling Microscopy (STM) was another major area of graduate research expertise. I used STM to characterize surfaces and ultrathin films at atomic-scale resolution.

  • Atomic-resolution surface imaging
  • Surface morphology and topography
  • Atomic structure and lattice measurements
  • Identification of different surface and material phases
  • Step heights and nanoscale dimensional measurements
  • Scanning Tunneling Spectroscopy and local I-V measurements
  • Electronic behavior with atomic-scale spatial precision
  • Correlation of deposition conditions with surface structure

STM provided a direct connection between how a thin film was grown and the atomic structure that actually formed on the surface.

I worked closely with the optical-metrology function and served as a technical backup and first line of support when measurement systems or results required additional troubleshooting.

  • PerkinElmer LAMBDA optical spectrometers
  • Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy
  • J.A. Woollam M-200 spectroscopic ellipsometry
  • J.A. Woollam VASE ellipsometry systems
  • Optical transmission measurements
  • Optical reflection measurements
  • Thin-film thickness characterization
  • Surface/interface roughness evaluation through optical modeling
  • Absorption and optical-property evaluation
  • Comparison of measured spectral performance with coating design
  • Process-development and production troubleshooting

Measurement-System Troubleshooting & Technical Ownership

My industrial metrology responsibilities were not limited to collecting measurements. When optical measurement equipment was not behaving correctly, I was frequently involved in determining whether the issue came from the sample, measurement method, calibration, software, or the instrument itself.

  • First-line troubleshooting of optical measurement equipment
  • Instrument performance verification
  • Calibration support
  • Measurement-method troubleshooting
  • Review of questionable or unexpected results
  • Technical support when the primary metrology resource was unavailable
  • Coordination of equipment repair when issues exceeded internal support
  • Interpretation of metrology results for manufacturing engineers and operators

The combination of process-engineering knowledge and measurement-system knowledge was especially useful because an unusual measurement does not automatically mean the instrument is wrong — it may be identifying a real process or coating problem.

  • Sample exposure and post-test evaluation

These measurements supported coating qualification, process development, Root Cause and Corrective Action (RCCA), and verification of manufacturing performance.

Measurement Science & Physics Foundation

My metrology background is strengthened by a physics and materials-science foundation that helps connect an instrument reading with the underlying physical behavior producing that result.

Surface science, solid-state and condensed-matter physics, quantum mechanics, electronic states, optics, materials science, and vacuum physics all became practical tools for interpreting measurement data.

This is particularly important in advanced manufacturing and research, where the value of metrology is not simply obtaining a number. The measurement must be understood well enough to determine whether a change represents real material behavior, a process shift, a sample-preparation problem, or a measurement-system issue.

From Measurement to Engineering Decision

Across research and manufacturing, I have used metrology to answer practical technical questions: Did the material grow correctly? What is its composition? What electronic structure formed? Is the surface ordered? Is the coating the correct thickness? Does it meet the optical design? Did the process change? Will the coating survive its environment?

My strongest metrology experience combines measurement technique, equipment knowledge, physics, process engineering, and troubleshooting. That allows characterization data to become an engineering decision rather than simply a measurement report.
