Research Impact & Field Recognition

One of my major research contributions was the experimental observation of strong room-temperature ferromagnetism in atomically thin vanadium selenide systems.

At the time, experimental two-dimensional magnets had largely been associated with magnetic ordering at substantially lower temperatures. Demonstrating robust magnetic behavior near and above room temperature expanded the possibilities being considered for two-dimensional magnetic materials and spin-based devices.

The work became part of the early experimental foundation of room-temperature 2D magnetism and helped establish vanadium selenides as an important research direction within the emerging field.

Room-Temperature Magnetism in Atomically Thin Materials

My first-author research demonstrated strong room-temperature ferromagnetic behavior in ultrathin vanadium selenide films grown on van der Waals substrates.

The research required development of controlled Molecular Beam Epitaxy (MBE) growth in the monolayer and sub-monolayer regimes, protection of air-sensitive samples, and correlation of structural, electronic, and magnetic measurements across multiple experimental techniques.

  • Synthesis of ultrathin and monolayer vanadium selenide
  • Room-temperature magnetic characterization
  • Thickness-dependent magnetic behavior
  • Substrate-dependent behavior
  • Atomic-scale structural characterization
  • Electronic-structure measurements
  • Comparison with theoretical predictions
  • Multi-technique investigation of the origin of the observed magnetism

Following the Evidence Beyond the Initial Discovery

An important part of research leadership is continuing to test an interpretation after the initial result is published.

Subsequent work investigated the origin of the magnetic behavior more deeply. By combining composition, atomic structure, electronic structure, annealing studies, and magnetic measurements, I found strong evidence connecting the observed magnetism with changes in vanadium-selenide composition and the formation of self-intercalated structures.

This work demonstrated how subtle differences in composition and atomic arrangement can substantially change the physical properties of an ultrathin material and provided an important refinement to the original interpretation of the system.

  • Controlled post-growth annealing experiments
  • Composition tracking through XPS
  • Atomic structure and surface-phase identification through STM
  • Magnetic-property correlation with structural transformation
  • High-resolution synchrotron characterization
  • TEM and diffraction support through collaborating laboratories
  • Comparison of experimental observations with theoretical calculations

Composition-Driven Phase Transformation in Ultrathin Films

Another major research contribution involved identifying how post-growth thermal processing changes the composition and atomic structure of ultrathin vanadium and titanium selenides.

The work connected selenium loss, atomic rearrangement, new superstructures, and the formation of self-intercalated compounds. Multiple characterization techniques were combined to distinguish structural changes that could otherwise be incorrectly interpreted from morphology alone.

Charge-Density-Wave Physics & Tunable Electronic States

My research also investigated Charge Density Waves (CDW), a collective electronic and structural state found in several low-dimensional materials.

In monolayer TiSe₂, we studied how reducing dimensionality changes the CDW state and how that state can be modified through substrate selection and electron doping.

  • Layer-dependent CDW behavior
  • Substrate-dependent electronic interactions
  • Temperature-dependent energy-gap measurements
  • Scanning Tunneling Spectroscopy measurements
  • Angle-Resolved Photoemission Spectroscopy
  • Electron-doping experiments
  • Suppression and tuning of collective electronic states

Research Mindset Beyond Academia

The most transferable part of my research background is not a particular material or instrument. It is the ability to enter a technically difficult problem where the answer is not known, determine what information is required, build a structured experimental approach, challenge assumptions, and follow the evidence until the problem is understood.

That same approach now supports my work in manufacturing, process engineering, quality, data analysis, systems implementation, and technical leadership.
