Quality Leadership Through Process Engineering

My quality experience developed through direct ownership of manufacturing processes and equipment where I was responsible for preventing defects, investigating failures, controlling process changes, and demonstrating that corrective actions actually improved performance.

In organizations where dedicated quality resources were limited, I also helped establish quality structure, reviewed manufacturing dispositions for consistency with approved processes and MES controls, supported quality-system development, and later helped onboard and train dedicated quality personnel.

The result is a practical quality perspective that combines process engineering, manufacturing systems, data, equipment knowledge, and formal quality methods.

Root Cause & Corrective Action

Root Cause and Corrective Action has been a major part of my manufacturing work. I have led and supported investigations involving process, equipment, cleaning, inspection, material, environmental, and measurement-related failures.

At Lockheed Martin, the formal RCCA methodology used 4-Blocker reports rather than fishbone diagrams. These reports provided a structured mechanism for documenting the problem, investigation, root cause, corrective action, and verification.

  • Process excursions and manufacturing defects
  • Thin-film and optical coating failures
  • Delamination and adhesion problems
  • Spectral-performance deviations
  • Cleaning-related defects
  • Inspection and handling issues
  • Equipment and chamber problems
  • Vacuum leaks and contamination
  • Temperature and calibration issues
  • Environmental and durability failures
  • Material and witness-sample abnormalities

Defect Reduction & Sustained Process Performance

One of my strongest quality results came from taking responsibility for a manufacturing area that initially experienced multiple defects per week across several tools and process steps.

The failures were not caused by a single mechanism. They included equipment, process, cleaning, inspection, and other manufacturing contributors, requiring a combination of RCCA, process control, operator support, equipment improvement, and sustained follow-up.

7 Months
Before leaving the organization, my primary manufacturing scope reached approximately seven consecutive months without a defect attributed to the processes, equipment, cleaning activities, or inspections under my responsibility.

The improvement changed the nature of recurring quality meetings. Reviews that previously required discussion of active failures eventually became brief confirmations of continued zero-defect performance for that manufacturing scope.

Quality Metrics, Reporting & Performance Visibility

Sustainable quality improvement requires historical visibility. Without trend data, an organization may know what failed today but cannot clearly determine whether the process is actually improving.

I pushed for stronger historical defect tracking and quality dashboards so manufacturing performance could be measured over time and improvements could be demonstrated objectively.

  • Recurring quality reports
  • 4-Blocker RCCA reporting
  • Defect and failure tracking
  • Historical quality-performance trending
  • Dashboard development and visualization
  • Weekly quality-review participation
  • Process and equipment performance metrics
  • Verification of corrective-action effectiveness

SPC, OCAP & Structured Failure Response

As the department Statistical Process Control (SPC) lead, I also developed the response structure used when a process triggered statistical-control rules.

The objective was to prevent an SPC alarm from becoming merely another notification. Engineers and supervisors needed a defined decision path for containment, troubleshooting, product disposition, escalation, corrective action, and prevention of recurrence.

Example of the SPC failure-response process I developed linking Statistical Process Control, Western Electric rules, Out-of-Control Action Plans, Nonconformance Reports, RCCA, and PFMEA updates.

  • Western Electric (WECO) statistical-rule response
  • Out-of-Control Action Plan (OCAP) workflow
  • Product containment and hold decisions
  • Repeat measurement and verification
  • Process and equipment troubleshooting
  • Engineering and supervisor escalation
  • Nonconformance Report (NCR) initiation
  • Root Cause and Corrective Action
  • Corrective and preventive action implementation
  • PFMEA update following identified failure mechanisms

Process Failure Mode & Effects Analysis

I developed the first department-wide Process Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (PFMEA) for a new semiconductor manufacturing organization.

Rather than limiting the analysis to one piece of equipment or one process step, the PFMEA covered the department's manufacturing tools and processes and created a common framework for identifying potential failure modes, evaluating risk, and defining preventive controls.

  • Process failure-mode identification
  • Potential effect evaluation
  • Potential cause identification
  • Existing prevention and detection controls
  • Risk prioritization
  • Recommended corrective and preventive actions
  • Connection between real manufacturing failures and PFMEA updates

The PFMEA was designed as a living process-control document rather than a one-time compliance exercise.

Change Control & Manufacturing Approval

Manufacturing quality also depends on controlling what is allowed to change after a process has been qualified.

I served as an approval member of the Change Review Board responsible for reviewing proposed manufacturing changes. My approval represented the manufacturing function, while I also provided technical feedback on the potential process impact of proposed changes.

  • Review of proposed manufacturing-process changes
  • Manufacturing approval authority within the review board
  • Technical evaluation of potential process impact
  • Assessment of quality and production risk
  • Verification that approved changes were properly controlled
  • Coordination with engineering and quality stakeholders

Manufacturing Risk, Safety & Equipment Readiness

Risk control has also included equipment safety and operational readiness. In a newly established manufacturing department, a large number of tools required formal safety assessments before the equipment could be fully incorporated into the controlled manufacturing environment.

I worked with Environmental, Health and Safety personnel to evaluate equipment and document risks such as pinch points, required labeling, chemical storage, and other equipment-specific hazards.

  • Approximately 50 equipment safety assessments completed during a compressed implementation period
  • Tool-by-tool hazard review
  • Pinch-point identification
  • Safety and warning-label verification
  • Chemical-storage and handling review
  • Equipment-readiness documentation
  • Support for asset creation and preventive-maintenance setup
  • Coordination with Environmental, Health and Safety

This work received internal recognition for its contribution to equipment safety and manufacturing readiness.

Quality as an Engineering System

I do not view quality as a final inspection performed after manufacturing. Effective quality starts with process definition, equipment readiness, risk identification, measurement, process control, disciplined change management, and rapid response when something behaves abnormally.

My experience allows me to work across that full loop — from detecting a problem, through technical investigation and root cause, to implementing the process or system changes needed to prevent it from happening again.
